# Chapter 9 — Buildings, Facilities, and the Quiet Majority

*(draft v1, 2026-08-30 — written by claude-fable-5, RogerAI Labs. Published sources carry `[R#]` markers resolved in the References; claims drawn from this lab's own runs are labeled as declared observations with their apparatus.)*

Every earlier chapter in this book pointed a camera at something dramatic: a robot arm, a moving vehicle, a turbine, a substation with a regional blast radius. Those are the machines that get photographed for the vendor deck. They are not the machines you will spend your career next to. The overwhelming majority of the world's networked equipment is boring on purpose — an air handler on a roof, a chilled-water pump in a basement, a variable-air-volume box above a ceiling tile, a walk-in freezer's compressor, an elevator controller, a lab fume hood, a nurse-call panel. This equipment shares three properties that make it the real volume market for machine-reading language models, and the same three properties make it the hardest place to deploy one carelessly.

First, there is a staggering amount of it. Commercial buildings in the United States alone cover roughly 97 billion square feet across about 6 million buildings, and heating, ventilation, air conditioning, and lighting dominate their energy use `[R54]`. A single mid-size office tower carries thousands of monitored points; a hospital or a semiconductor fab carries tens of thousands. The count of *devices* under building automation dwarfs the count of industrial robots by orders of magnitude, and every one of them emits text-shaped telemetry that nobody has time to read.

Second, this equipment only gets human attention when it fails, and by design it is supposed to fail rarely. That is the quiet-majority paradox: the equipment is important precisely because it is invisible, and it is invisible precisely because it usually works. A facilities team of a dozen people is responsible for a portfolio that generates more alarms in a week than they could read in a year. They triage by fire — whatever is loudest, hottest, or complained about — and everything else runs on hope and a quarterly filter change.

Third — and this is the property this chapter turns on — the data is *almost* structured. Building automation has spent forty years digitizing itself, and the result is a world of protocols, object models, and points lists that look like clean machine-readable data and are, in practice, a swamp of inconsistent names, dead points, hand-entered abbreviations, and override flags that mean the opposite of what they say. It is the perfect environment for a language model to be useful and the perfect environment for one to be confidently wrong. This chapter is about telling those two outcomes apart.

## What "building automation" actually is

Strip away the marketing and a building automation system (BAS, sometimes BMS for "management") is a hierarchy of controllers talking a small number of field protocols, exposing every sensor and actuator as an addressable *point*. A point is not much: it has a name, a current value, a set of properties describing units and status, and — if you are lucky — a description string somebody typed during commissioning. The controller runs the actual control loops (a PID keeping a supply-air temperature at setpoint); the network exposes the loop's inputs and outputs so a supervisory layer, and now a model, can read them.

The lingua franca of this world is BACnet, standardized as ASHRAE Standard 135 and adopted internationally as ISO 16484-5 `[R49]`. BACnet is worth understanding in a little depth because it is genuinely well designed as a machine-readable protocol, and because its design decisions are exactly the ones that determine what a language model can and cannot safely conclude from it.

BACnet models everything as **objects** with **properties**. An analog-input object representing a temperature sensor has a `Present_Value` (the reading), a `Units` property (an enumerated value — degrees Celsius is enumeration 62), a `Reliability` property, an `Out_Of_Service` flag, and a `Status_Flags` bitstring, among dozens of others. The two properties that matter most for honest reading are `Reliability` and `Out_Of_Service`, and almost nobody outside the controls trade knows they exist.

`Reliability` is an enumeration with roughly twenty-five defined values — `no-fault-detected`, `no-sensor`, `over-range`, `under-range`, `open-loop`, `shorted-loop`, `unreliable-other`, and so on `[R49]`. This is the building world's version of the NAMUR NE 107 diagnostic taxonomy that the process industries use — Failure, Function Check, Out of Specification, Maintenance Required `[R55]` — and it is the single most important field a model can read on a building point, because it tells you whether the number next to it can be trusted at all. A supply-air sensor reading 55 °F with `Reliability = shorted-loop` is not reading 55 °F. It is reading nothing, and the 55 is the value the input holds when the loop is shorted. A model that reports "supply air is 55 °F" from that object has just laundered a hardware failure into a confident sensor reading — the exact clean-JSON lie Chapter 3 warned about, wearing a standardized field name.

`Out_Of_Service` is subtler and more dangerous. When a technician sets a point out of service, the `Present_Value` stops tracking the physical world and becomes writable for testing — you can force it to any number you like to exercise the downstream logic. A point left out of service after a service call reads a *frozen, fictional* value that looks completely normal. There is no way to detect this from the value alone; you must read the flag. This is not a rare edge case. In any large building, at any given moment, some nonzero number of points are out of service and forgotten, and a model that ignores the flag will build a coherent, confident, wrong story out of them.

There is one more BACnet mechanism that will bite any naive reader: the **priority array** on commandable objects. When you tell an air handler's outside-air damper to open, you do not write a value; you write a value *at a priority level*, one of sixteen. The object's actual commanded value is the highest-priority non-null entry in the array. Life-safety systems command at priority 1; a manual operator override sits around priority 8; the normal control program writes at priority 16. This means the *commanded* position of an actuator and the position the control logic *thinks* it wrote can differ, silently, because a higher-priority override is in force. "The damper is commanded 20% open" and "the control program wants the damper 80% open" can both be true simultaneously. A model that reads only the effective value, or only the program's output, tells half the story — and in a comfort complaint or an energy investigation, the half it drops is usually the half that explains the problem.

BACnet is the dominant protocol but far from the only one. **LonWorks** (ISO/IEC 14908) still runs a large installed base, particularly in older and European buildings, with its own network-variable model. **KNX** (ISO/IEC 14543-3) owns much of the European lighting, shading, and room-control market `[R53]`. **Modbus** — Chapter 4's bare register world — shows up constantly at the equipment level, under a BACnet or LON gateway, exposing a chiller's or a boiler's internals as raw registers with no self-description at all. And increasingly the supervisory and analytics layers speak **MQTT** `[R18]`, often with the **Sparkplug** specification `[R17]` layered on top to give the topic namespace and payloads the birth/death and metric-definition structure that raw MQTT lacks. A single real building routinely speaks three or four of these at once, bridged by gateways of varying quality, and the model that reads the building has to survive all of them.

## The naming problem is the real problem

Here is the thing nobody tells you before your first BAS integration: the protocols are the easy part. BACnet is a good protocol. The hard part is that the *points have no reliable names*.

A point that measures the discharge air temperature of the third air handler on the fourth floor might be labeled `AHU-4-3.DAT`, or `SF4_AH3_DisTmp`, or `4.3.SA-T`, or `RTU34DAT`, or `AI:1047`, or a commissioning technician's initials plus a number they were tracking in a notebook that no longer exists. Multiply that by ten thousand points, across three controls contractors who worked the building over fifteen years, each with their own house convention, none documented. This is not a hypothetical; it is the normal state of the installed base. The controls industry has a name for the labor of fixing it — "point mapping" or "tagging" — and it is the single largest cost line in every analytics and fault-detection project, routinely more than half the budget.

The industry's response has been to build semantic layers that sit above the raw points and describe what each one *is* in a machine-readable vocabulary. Three are worth knowing:

**Project Haystack** `[R51]` is a tagging convention: each point gets a set of tags (`discharge`, `air`, `temp`, `sensor`) plus references to the equipment and site it belongs to. It is pragmatic, widely adopted in the analytics vendor space, and loose enough to be applied inconsistently — which means Haystack tags are themselves data a model must read skeptically.

**Brick** `[R52]` is a formal ontology — an RDF/OWL schema with a defined class hierarchy and relationships (a `Discharge_Air_Temperature_Sensor` `isPointOf` an `AHU` which `feeds` a `VAV` which `hasLocation` a `Room`). Brick's ambition is a query-able graph of the whole building, so you can ask "every VAV downstream of AHU-4-3" and get an answer without knowing anyone's naming convention. It is rigorous and, for exactly that reason, laborious to populate.

**ASHRAE Standard 223P** is the standards body's own semantic data model, developed to give building metadata the same official footing BACnet gave building communication `[R49]`. It is newer and less deployed than Brick or Haystack as of this writing, and I will not pretend to more field experience with it than exists.

The reason to care about all this in a book about language models is that **the naming and tagging problem is the single best-fit task for a machine-reading model in the entire building domain, and also the one most likely to produce confident nonsense.** A model is genuinely good at looking at `SF4_AH3_DisTmp` alongside its units (°F), its typical range (52–60 during cooling), its parent equipment, and a handful of sibling points, and proposing: *this is very likely the discharge-air-temperature sensor for air handler 3, floor 4.* That is real, valuable work — it is the expensive human labor of point mapping, accelerated. But it is exactly the kind of task where a model will also confidently map `SF4_AH3_DisTmp` to *supply fan status* because "SF" pattern-completes to "supply fan," when SF here was the initials of the contractor. The task and its worst failure mode are the same shape. Everything in this chapter about abstention exists because of tasks like this one.

## Where a model actually earns its place

Set against the quiet-majority backdrop, the useful applications are not exotic. They are the unglamorous middle of the facilities workday, and they cluster into a handful of patterns you will recognize from the earlier domain chapters, specialized to buildings.

**Points-list normalization and tagging.** The task above: proposing a semantic mapping for a raw points list, with a confidence and the evidence it used, into a human review queue. The model never writes a tag directly into the graph; it drafts a mapping a controls engineer approves or corrects. Every correction is a training example (Chapter 7's loop) and every approval is progress against the mapping backlog that is throttling every downstream project. Framed this way, the model is not "understanding the building" — it is proposing normalizations that a human accepts under their own name, which is the only framing that survives contact with the fact that it will sometimes be wrong.

**Fault detection and diagnostics triage.** Automated fault detection and diagnostics (FDD) for building systems is a mature field with two decades of published methods — rule-based, model-based, and data-driven — surveyed thoroughly by Katipamula and Brambley `[R56]`. The important thing to understand is that **the model's job here is not to replace the FDD engine; it is to make the FDD engine's output legible.** A good FDD ruleset ("simultaneous heating and cooling," "economizer not economizing when it should," "sensor drift against a virtual reference") fires deterministic, explainable alarms. What it does not do is prioritize them against a facilities team's actual capacity, group the seventeen alarms that are all one stuck damper, or write the two sentences a technician needs to decide whether this is a today problem or a next-quarter problem. That translation and triage layer is where language genuinely helps, and it sits *on top of* deterministic detection, never replacing it. The rule decides there is a fault; the model helps a human decide what to do about it. Keep that division of labor and you get most of the value with a fraction of the risk; blur it — ask the model to *detect* the fault from raw trends — and you have re-created the R.26 problem from this lab's own bench, discussed below, where general models read machine data at chance.

**Alarm and event summarization.** Buildings generate alarm floods for the same reasons plants do (Chapter 8), and the same alarm-management discipline applies — the ISA-18.2 / IEC 62682 framework of prioritization, shelving, and rationalization `[R32]`. A model that turns "312 alarms overnight" into "one chilled-water plant tripped at 02:14 and cascaded; 280 of the 312 alarms are downstream of that single event; 32 are unrelated, of which these 3 look independent" is doing the shift-handoff summarization job Chapter 5's plant chapter described, on a building's data. The value is entirely in the grouping and the honesty about what is and isn't downstream of the root event — and the danger is entirely in a confident causal story the alarm timestamps do not actually support.

**Occupant-complaint mapping.** "It's freezing in the northeast conference room on 4." This is a natural-language input — a genuine one, from a human — that has to be mapped to equipment: which VAV box serves that room, what is its current setpoint and airflow, is its reheat valve commanded and is it responding, is the damper in the priority array overridden. This is the task where a model's language ability and its building-graph reading meet, and it is a lovely fit *if* the graph it is reading is trustworthy and *if* it reports the override state honestly rather than the effective value alone.

**Work-order and manual assistance.** The Chapter 5 pattern, verbatim, on building equipment: drafting CMMS work orders from a fault, pulling the relevant procedure from a manual for a specific rooftop unit, recalling the model-specific fault-code meaning. This is the safest and often highest-value application because its authority ceiling is a *draft a human approves*, and buildings have a mature CMMS culture to receive those drafts.

Notice what is absent from this list: the model does not tune a control loop, does not write a setpoint, does not reset an alarm, does not command a damper. In the building world as everywhere else in this book, the frontier between reading and acting is a hard line, and it lands well short of the BAS's command interface. Chapter 11 makes the argument in full; here it is enough to say that the priority array is not a place a probabilistic component gets a slot.

## The clean-JSON lie, building edition

Chapter 3 established the general principle: a well-formed data object can lie about a dirty sensor. Buildings have a rich, specific vocabulary of these lies, and a model deployed here has to be trained against every one of them.

**The out-of-service freeze.** Covered above and worth repeating because it is the most common: a forced point reads a plausible, stable, completely fictional value. The only defense is reading `Out_Of_Service` and treating a set flag as a hard abstain on that point's value — "AHU-4-3 discharge temp reads 55 °F but the point is out of service; this value is not live."

**The unreliable-but-present reading.** The `Reliability` enum is non-`no-fault-detected` but the `Present_Value` still holds a number. Same defense: the reliability flag dominates the value, always.

**The stale COV.** BACnet's change-of-value subscriptions mean a value updates only when it changes by more than a threshold. A sensor that has genuinely flatlined (a real fault) and a sensor that is legitimately steady look identical in the data — both stop sending updates. Without the `Reliability` flag or an independent liveness check, "the value hasn't changed in six hours" is ambiguous between "stable" and "dead," and the model must say so rather than pick.

**The override that inverts meaning.** The priority-array case: the effective value is real but is the product of an override the control logic did not intend, so any conclusion about "why the system is doing this" that ignores the override is wrong at the causal level even when it is right at the value level.

**The commissioning artifact.** Points that exist in the network but were never wired, or were wired to the wrong terminal, and have read a constant since installation. These are indistinguishable from a working steady point until you look at the history and notice it has *never* moved, or cross-check against a physically coupled point that does move.

**The unit trap.** BACnet carries a `Units` enumeration, but gateways and hand-configured points get it wrong constantly — a point in kPa labeled psi, a delta-T labeled as an absolute temperature, a percentage labeled in engineering units. A model doing arithmetic across points (computing an efficiency, a delta, an approach temperature) inherits every unit error silently. The defense is to treat the declared units as a claim to be sanity-checked against the value's range, not as ground truth — and to abstain from cross-point arithmetic when the units do not reconcile.

The through-line of all six: **the value is the least trustworthy field on a building point, and the status metadata is the most trustworthy, and the entire industry's tooling foregrounds the value and buries the metadata.** A machine-reading model that inverts that emphasis — that reads `Reliability`, `Out_Of_Service`, `Status_Flags`, and the priority array *first* and the `Present_Value` last — is doing something the existing dashboards mostly do not, and that inversion is a large fraction of the value it can add. It is also, not coincidentally, the discipline that keeps it honest.

## A measured caution from this lab's bench

I want to be concrete about the failure mode, because it is the load-bearing reason this chapter keeps insisting the model triage rather than diagnose. This lab ran a direct test of whether general-purpose language models can read machine data — the deterministic feature summaries of sensor channels — and classify their fault state.

The apparatus, so you can weigh it: 300 simulated sensor channels balanced at exactly 50 per fault class across a six-way fault taxonomy (`none`, `stuck`, `dropout`, `drifting`, `noisy`, `railed`), a fixed random seed, byte-reproducible inputs. The decoder was enum-scoring, not free generation — every field a closed enum, each allowed value scored by its summed token log-probability and the argmax taken, which is exactly deterministic and structurally cannot emit an invalid value. The feature semantics were spelled out in the system prompt (for example, "a large head-slope with a zero tail-slope means the signal moved then stopped, which is *stuck*"). Models tested spanned a hundred-fold parameter range, from a 270M model through a 27B one.

The result: **every model landed at or below chance.** Chance on the six-way balanced task is 16.7%. The 270M model scored 0.0%, predicting a single value for all 300 channels; the 0.8B model scored 16.7% by answering `none` every time (the flat-prior score, not partial capability); the 27B model scored 14.3%, *below* chance. The control that makes this interpretable: roughly thirty lines of hand-written if-statements over the identical feature summary scored 63.3% `[LAB: RogerAI Labs bench, RESULTS-MATRIX R.26, 2026-08-11; 300-channel balanced set, enum-scoring decoder, seed-reproducible]`.

I am not reporting this to disparage the models — it is a task nobody trained them for, posed in a form nobody had posed. I am reporting it because it is the empirical floor under this chapter's central design rule. A general model handed raw or lightly-summarized machine data and asked to *diagnose* will produce fluent, confident, chance-level answers, and the enum-scoring will make them look tidy. The thirty-line ruleset beats it because fault detection from clean features is a *deterministic* problem that deterministic code solves. So: let the deterministic FDD engine detect, and let the model do the thing it is actually good at — reading the resulting fault, the equipment context, the manual, and the history, and writing the honest human-facing summary with its uncertainty attached. The division of labor is not a stylistic preference. It is what the measurement says.

## The typed-fault output, for buildings

Chapter 4 argued that a model reading machines should emit a *typed assertion with a confidence margin*, not a paragraph. Buildings give this argument an unusually clean home because the vocabulary already exists. NAMUR NE 107's four-state diagnostic classification — Failure, Function Check, Out of Specification, Maintenance Required `[R55]` — and BACnet's own `Reliability` and `Event_State` enumerations `[R49]` mean the model's output does not have to be free text; it can be a selection from a standardized, closed set that the CMMS and the operator already understand.

Concretely, a building fault assertion should carry: the equipment reference (resolved against the semantic graph, with its own confidence), the fault class from a closed enum, the evidence points and their status flags, a confidence margin, and — this is the field that keeps it honest — an explicit abstain or escalate arm for the cases where the status metadata says the underlying data cannot support any assertion at all. A model that reads a shorted-loop sensor and emits `{equipment: AHU-4-3, assertion: ABSTAIN, reason: primary evidence point unreliable (shorted-loop), needed: verify sensor at the device}` has done more good than one that emits a confident diagnosis from a dead sensor, and the closed-enum output form is what makes that abstention a first-class, selectable, auditable outcome rather than a sentence the model has to remember to write. This is Chapter 11's machinery previewed on building data; the point here is that the building standards hand you the enums for free.

## The organizational reality: the quiet failure economics

The technical fit is only half the story. The other half is why buildings are underserved despite being the volume market, and it is an economics problem the model has to be deployed *into*, not around.

A facilities organization does not fail loudly. A chiller that runs 12% less efficiently than it should does not trip an alarm; it just costs money, quietly, every hour, until an energy audit two years later notices. A VAV box stuck in reheat does not page anyone; it fights the cooling system in one zone and shows up as a comfort complaint and a utility bill nobody itemizes to it. The building domain is defined by faults that are individually small, collectively enormous, and structurally invisible — the classic "quiet failure." The published FDD literature exists precisely because this invisible-loss category is large and addressable `[R56]`, and the reason it stays large is not that the faults are undetectable but that *detecting them produces more findings than any facilities team can act on.*

This changes what "deploying a model" means in a building. In a plant, the model helps a technician answer a question they are already asking. In a building, the model's harder job is to help a team *decide which of a thousand quiet problems to spend this week's four available hours on*. That is a prioritization and triage problem — dollars-per-year of waste, occupant impact, failure-progression risk — and it is exactly the kind of judgment where a model's confident wrongness is most expensive, because the team has no capacity to catch a misprioritization. Send them to tear into a healthy air handler because the model misread an out-of-service point as a fault, and you have not just wasted the four hours; you have spent the credibility that the next, correct recommendation needed. Chapter 5's asymmetric-cost argument is, if anything, sharper here than on the plant floor, because the human check has less slack.

The workflow consequence, which mirrors Chapter 8's, is that **every model output in a building lands in the CMMS as a draft work order with its evidence and confidence attached, ranked, into a queue a human owns** — and that the abstentions and low-confidence items route to a "needs better data" backlog that is itself a map of the building's points-mapping and sensor-health debt. Track the abstentions by cause and you get, for free, a ranked list of the sensors worth recommissioning and the points worth remapping, ordered by how often reality asks for them. The refusal is not a dead end; it is the facilities team's capital-planning input.

## The verticals inside the quiet majority

"Buildings" is not one domain; it is a family of them that share BACnet and diverge sharply in what a quiet failure *costs*, and the divergence changes where a model earns its place and how conservative it must be. Four are worth naming because they bracket the range.

**Commercial office and retail** is the base case this chapter has mostly assumed: comfort complaints, energy waste, and equipment longevity, where a quiet failure costs money and comfort but rarely safety. This is where a model's triage-and-draft role is most straightforwardly valuable and where the tolerance for a low-stakes wrong suggestion is highest — a misprioritized air handler wastes a technician's morning, not more.

**Healthcare** raises every stake. Hospitals run the same BACnet equipment plus pressure relationships (an operating room must stay positively pressured, an isolation room negatively), temperature-controlled pharmacy and specimen storage, and life-safety systems with regulatory logging requirements. A quiet failure here — a pressure relationship silently inverted, a vaccine fridge drifting — has patient-safety and compliance consequences, and the model's abstention discipline is correspondingly stricter: reading the `Reliability` and `Out_Of_Service` flags on a pressure or temperature point is not good practice here, it is the difference between a real alert and a laundered failure of a point someone forced during a service call.

**Laboratories and cleanrooms** add fume-hood face velocity, particle counts, and tight environmental envelopes where the equipment protects both people and product. The semiconductor and pharmaceutical fabs at the extreme of this vertical carry tens of thousands of points and lose enormous sums to environmental excursions — and, like the process plants of Chapter 8, they often run under change-control regimes strict enough that even a *suggested* setpoint change goes through formal review. The model's role narrows toward reading and summarizing, and its authority ceiling drops accordingly.

**Cold chain and data centers** are the verticals where the equipment *is* the product's survival. A walk-in freezer's compressor or a data hall's cooling loop failing quietly is a direct loss event, and both run dense telemetry that is a natural fit for continuous machine reading. Data centers in particular have embraced building-analytics tooling early precisely because the failure cost is legible in dollars per minute — which makes them, not the showcase office tower, the place the quiet-majority argument is easiest to fund.

The through-line: the *technical* fit of a machine-reading model is roughly constant across these verticals because they share the protocol and the data shape, but the *authority ceiling and abstention strictness* tighten as the blast radius grows — from comfort, to compliance, to product, to safety. A deployment that copies an office-tower configuration into a hospital without tightening the abstention floor and lowering the authority ceiling has misread the vertical, and the standardized status metadata that lets it abstain honestly is exactly the same in both; only the cost of ignoring it has changed.

## Commissioning data: the model's best and most dangerous teacher

One more building-specific source deserves naming because it is simultaneously the richest evidence a building model has and the most treacherous: the commissioning and as-built record. When a building's controls are installed and tuned, a commissioning process generates points lists, sequences of operation, submittal documents, and functional-test records — the closest thing a building has to a manual. A model reading a building leans on this record constantly: it is where the semantic meaning of a cryptic point name is often recoverable, where the intended sequence of operation (against which "is the economizer economizing when it should?" is even a well-posed question) is written down, and where the equipment schedule lives.

The danger is that the commissioning record is a *snapshot at installation* and the building is a living thing. Points get rewired, sequences get overridden in the field, equipment gets swapped, and the as-built documentation drifts from reality on the schedule of every undocumented service call. A model that treats the commissioning record as ground truth will confidently explain the building it was *designed* to be rather than the building it *is* — and the two diverge most in exactly the old, much-modified buildings where the model's help is most valuable. The discipline mirrors the sensor-value discipline: the commissioning record is a strong prior, not a fact, and where it conflicts with live telemetry (the sequence says the damper should modulate; the priority array shows it overridden fixed), the conflict is a finding to surface, not a discrepancy to resolve in favor of the paperwork. Handled that way, the drift between the as-built record and the live system becomes one more thing the model is uniquely positioned to *find* — the documentation debt of the building, located for free — rather than a trap it falls into.

## The read/suggest/act line, drawn in a building

Because the temptation is strongest here — a BAS is *right there*, commandable over the same network the model is already reading — the boundary deserves a building-specific statement.

**Read** is the model consuming points, status flags, histories, and the semantic graph. This is unrestricted and is where nearly all the value lives.

**Suggest** is the model drafting: a work order, a points-mapping, a triage ranking, an alarm summary, a "you might check the economizer damper on AHU-4-3" recommendation. This is the ceiling of routine deployment, and everything it produces is badged as machine-drafted and lands in a queue a human dispositions.

**Act** — writing a setpoint, commanding a damper, resetting an alarm, forcing a point — is out of scope, full stop, and not because the model will never be capable enough. It is out of scope because the priority array exists, because a setpoint change in an occupied building has comfort and safety and code consequences, because the BAS's command path is the one interface where a probabilistic component's error becomes a physical event with no human in between, and because the building already has a change-management culture (setpoints are changed by controls engineers, logged, and reversible) that a model would have to *bypass* to act, not integrate with. The whole architecture of this book is that capability flows down toward the edge and authority does not cross the read/suggest line without a human. Buildings are where that line is most tempting to smudge and where smudging it is least defensible.

There is a narrow, legitimate exception worth naming so the rule stays credible: a model may participate in a *proposed* control change that goes through the building's normal engineering review — draft a revised schedule, propose a setpoint reset strategy, generate a sequence-of-operation change per ASHRAE Guideline 36's standardized high-performance sequences `[R50]` — as a document a controls engineer reviews, tests, and commits under their own authority. That is the model helping write the change, not making it. The distinction is the same one Chapter 8 drew and Chapter 11 will formalize: proposing a change into a review process is suggestion; effecting a change is action, and action stays with the human and the deterministic control logic.

## The integration seams, building specifics

Three adapters, mirroring Chapter 8's, specialized to the building stack.

**The BAS/graph seam.** The model reads the building through a gateway that owns the protocol details (BACnet, LON, Modbus-under-a-gateway, MQTT/Sparkplug) and — critically — the semantic layer (Haystack tags, Brick graph, or the raw points list with whatever mapping exists). The gateway's job is Chapter 4's rendering job: present the model a *typed, status-flag-forward* view of each point, not a raw `Present_Value`. This is also where the read-only posture is enforced physically: the gateway has read access to the BAS and no write path to any commandable object. The security review can verify that in one line, which is exactly the point.

**The CMMS seam.** The building's computerized maintenance management system is the destination for every draft work order, flagged machine-drafted, ranked by the triage confidence, into the normal approval queue. Buildings tend to have real CMMS discipline (regulatory drivers in healthcare and labs help), so this seam is often the most mature part of the deployment.

**The occupant/operator seam.** Two distinct surfaces: the occupant-facing complaint intake (natural language in, mapped to equipment, routed) and the operator-facing summary card (the alarm digest, the shift handoff, the current triage ranking, read-only). Keep them separate; the occupant surface accepts language and the operator surface only presents it, and neither is a command line into the BAS.

## A worked pattern: from complaint to honest triage

Tie it together on one realistic case, because the pattern is easier to copy than to describe.

The input is an occupant complaint: "Conference room 4-NE has been cold all week, especially mornings." The model's job is not to fix it; it is to produce a triage item a facilities technician can act on in ninety seconds instead of thirty minutes of point-hunting.

Step one, resolve the equipment. The model maps "conference room 4-NE" to a space in the semantic graph and finds the VAV box serving it — `VAV-4-17` — with a stated confidence and the evidence (the graph's `feeds`/`hasLocation` relationships, plus the space name match). If the graph is ambiguous — two VAVs could serve that space, the room name doesn't cleanly match — this is an abstain-and-ask, not a guess: *"I can't uniquely identify the box for 4-NE; candidates are VAV-4-17 and VAV-4-19. Which serves the northeast corner?"* That clarifying question is a different and correct output, per Chapter 5's grades of no.

Step two, read the box honestly. `VAV-4-17`: discharge-air temp, airflow setpoint versus measured, damper position, reheat valve command and position, zone temp versus setpoint. And — first, not last — the status flags on each: is the zone-temp sensor `Reliability = no-fault-detected`? Is the reheat valve command in the priority array, or is there an override? Is any point `Out_Of_Service`?

Step three, form a triage assertion, or abstain. Suppose the reheat valve is commanded 60% but its position feedback reads 0%, both points reliable, no override. That is a real, evidence-supported finding: *"VAV-4-17 reheat valve commanded 60% open but reading 0% position — consistent with a stuck or failed reheat valve or actuator, which would explain a cold northeast zone. Confidence: high. Evidence: valve command vs. position, both points live. Suggested action: inspect reheat valve/actuator on VAV-4-17."* Into the CMMS queue as a draft, ranked by occupant impact.

But suppose instead the zone-temp sensor reads `Reliability = open-loop`. Now the entire chain of reasoning rests on a dead sensor, and the honest output is: *"Cannot triage 4-NE from telemetry — the zone temperature sensor on VAV-4-17 reads open-loop (failed). The reported cold may be real or a sensor artifact. Suggested action: verify comfort on-site and check the zone sensor."* That is an abstention that still produces a work item, still names the missing evidence, and still costs the organization only a lookup — while a model that ignored the flag would have manufactured a confident diagnosis from nothing and sent someone to replace a working reheat valve.

The two branches diverge on exactly one thing: whether the model reads the status metadata before the value. That single discipline is most of the difference between a building-model deployment that earns trust and one that spends it.

## The chapter in one sentence

The quiet majority of the world's machines are boring building equipment whose data is *almost* structured — clean-looking values sitting on top of standardized status metadata that most tooling ignores — and a language model earns its place there by inverting that emphasis: reading reliability, out-of-service, and override state first, triaging and drafting rather than diagnosing and commanding, and treating the standardized abstain enums as a first-class outcome, so that "I can't tell from this data, and here is which sensor to fix" becomes the facilities team's capital-planning map instead of a shrug.

The next chapter leaves the domains behind and asks the question every one of these deployments eventually forces: given all of this, *where does the model physically run* — and what does each rung of that ladder cost you in latency, residency, and the version discipline that keeps a building's answers reproducible.
