## Introduction

This book is for people who put software next to machines: plant and controls engineers,
robot cell integrators, fleet and telematics leads, building-automation technicians,
grid and process operators, and the reliability engineers who get the ticket when any of
it fails. It assumes fluency with at least one machine domain — a historian, a CAN bus,
a teach pendant, a BACnet trunk, a relay panel — and no machine-learning research
background.

Its claim is broad in geography and narrow in method. Language models can read the
textual and semi-structured surfaces of the physical world — manuals, fault codes,
protocol frames, alarm floods, work orders — and they can do it on hardware you own.
They cannot be trusted with free-text authority next to equipment. The engineering that
makes them useful is the same engineering that makes them refuse to answer: schemas,
rungs, measurements, abstention, and a human who still owns the interlock.

This volume is Industrial Series Nº 3. Nº 1 made the plant-floor argument with a small
local model. Nº 2 made the physics argument for local inference. Nº 3 widens both into
the rest of the machine world without diluting the method. Boundaries are stated in
Chapter 1 in plain text. Numbers carry sources. The provenance page says exactly which
model wrote what, what grounded it, and which human must still verify it before it ships.
